Every plant I have worked in runs on data that has quietly stopped matching the material on the floor. Counts drift. Work in progress goes missing between the stock room and the machine. Offcuts get scrapped because no record says they exist. Damage is found weeks after it happened, in the wrong department, with no evidence left. Every decision downstream is then made against a fiction, and nobody making those decisions can tell.

Closing that gap is most of what I build. Some of it reconstructs the truth after the fact, which is the expensive way and usually the only way available. The better answer, and the direction the work has been moving, is to capture each event where and when it physically happens so there is nothing left to reconcile.

Three and a half years as a manufacturing engineer designing assembly lines and running time studies, then into procurement. Both jobs ran on spreadsheets that broke quietly and ERP screens nobody could get data out of. I started writing the tools myself, and that is now most of what I do.

Most of it runs inside a plant. Where a number appears below, I say how it was measured. Where something was not measured, I say that instead.

Inventory Database

A small manufacturer's entire material flow, running inside one Excel workbook. Free, and the code is public.

Open the browser preview No install, no signup. Works on a phone.

Raise a purchase order, prepare it as a PDF for the supplier, receive it into stock, move it to the production floor, record what was actually consumed, ship the finished goods. Every movement timestamped.

The reorder alert is the part worth looking at. It does not check whether stock sits below a minimum. It checks whether a product will run out before an order placed today could physically arrive, measuring its recorded daily usage against that supplier's lead time, and it drops anything already on order. That is the difference between an alert people act on and one they learn to ignore.

Moving material to production and consuming it are separate actions, because they are separate events. Stock leaving the stock room is relocated, not spent. Collapsing those two is the usual shortcut and it is why work in progress goes missing.

The scripts have a test suite that runs on every push. Office Scripts usually go untested because they only run inside Excel, so the tests stand in for the part of Excel they touch and then run the script files unmodified: what passes is the same file you import, not a copy adapted to be testable. Three of the tests exist to catch the numbering bug below if it is ever reintroduced, and they do.

What the preview is, and what it is not. It runs the real workflow and the real reorder logic, but entirely in browser memory. Refresh it and everything resets. It exists so you can see the thing work in ten seconds, not so you can run a plant on it. The system the inventory runs on

Seventy Office Scripts, around seventeen thousand lines of TypeScript, against one Excel workbook. It is what the plant's inventory and reporting actually run on day to day.

Office Scripts (TypeScript) · Excel on the web · ~17,000 lines across 70 scripts

It grew one script at a time, which is the honest account of how it was built. There was no design phase. What it settled into is four layers.

Capture. Counts, transfers, receipts and rejections go in through entry scripts rather than free typing, so every movement lands in one history table with a type and a timestamp. Purchase order data is read in from ERP exports rather than rekeyed.

Validation. This is the layer worth talking about. A count is checked against what is physically possible: the last count, plus everything logged as received, transferred or rejected since. A number above that ceiling means a record is wrong, not that the shelf is wrong, and the two need different people to fix them. Results split three ways. Definitely wrong, which is work. Shelf and paperwork disagree in a way the data cannot resolve, which is for reading and not for fixing. And nothing impossible found, which is not the same as right, and is labelled so it does not get read that way.

In practice it runs one category at a time, straight after that category's counts are entered. You pick the category, run the check, and it shows you how the stock behaved against everything recorded since the last count. What comes back is one of two jobs: go and look at the shelf again, because the count is wrong, or find the movement nobody entered, because the count is right and the paperwork is behind. Those need different people, which is why the check has to say which one it is rather than just flagging a number.

Analysis. Daily usage rates derived from movement history rather than typed in. Minimum stock levels rebuilt from what actually got received, replacing numbers nobody could source. Consumption forecasting across three product lines, so material is ordered against projected usage instead of in response to a stockout.

Reporting. Per-category reports built as HTML, exported, and scheduled to department managers for six in the morning. Low stock and purchasing reports off the same data.

Maintenance. A workbook this size drifts. Sheets get renamed, ranges stop being proper tables, templates fall out of date, and sheets nobody uses any more are left lying around. Every script above finds its data by table name, so when one of those things drifts the tool does not complain: it quietly reads the wrong range, or none at all. These scripts put the structure back. Unglamorous, and the reason the other four keep working.

What is wrong with it. It is a monolith living in a workbook, with one author, and that workbook is a single point of failure. That is a structural weakness, not a modest way of describing a strength, and the operating procedures below exist because of it. Whether the validation layer has actually reduced count error is unmeasured. I know it catches things. I do not know the rate.

Operating procedures, so it is not only mine

A system that runs a plant cannot depend on one person being reachable. Writing it down so other people can run it correctly is part of the job, not an afterthought.

Process design · one task sheet per role · purchasing, receiving, counting, production control

Work is isolated rather than trusted. Everyone writes into a working copy and the master is merged afterwards, so unfamiliar hands cannot corrupt the record they are writing into, because they are not writing into it.

Each role gets its own sheet, and each sheet says what the step is and then what happens if it is skipped. Closing a purchase order in the ERP while leaving it open in the workbook is called out as the one combination that causes trouble, because nothing alerts anyone and the numbers quietly stop agreeing. A rejection raised without a matching row on the shelf record is called out as the one failure in the whole process that leaves no trace, since it looks exactly like ordinary consumption to everything downstream. Telling somebody a rule is weaker than telling them what their mistake will look like to everyone else, which is usually nothing at all.

The instructions for reading the validation output are written the same way. Red is work. Amber is to be read and not fixed, because the disagreement has two believable explanations and you cannot tell them apart from a screen, so you walk out to the rack only when the size of the gap is not believable for that item. And in as many words: never invent a number to clear the table. A row left red is visible and can be fixed later. A correct count edited into a wrong one will never be flagged again.

Not yet proven. Parts of this have been run before: the cycle count job below was split up and handed to five people for two weeks. What has not happened is a handover of the whole thing at once, across every role, which is what these procedures cover and what runs for the first time in November 2026. So this is a design, not a result. Whether people follow a sheet when its author is not there to ask is the open question, and it is the only one that matters. I would rather publish the design now and the outcome afterwards than describe an untested plan as though it had worked.

Cutting stock optimizer

Extrusion profiles were cut order line by order line against 192 inch bars. No packing logic, and every offcut became scrap.

Python · first-fit-decreasing packing · SQLite · Tkinter

Cutting one order at a time is what wastes the material. Every bar is 192 inches, each order line needs its own set of shorter lengths, and whatever is left at the end of a bar is scrap, because nothing else on that line fits it. Planning all the lines together instead lets a short piece from one order fill the tail of a bar that was started for another.

The rule it follows is deliberately simple: take the longest piece still needed, put it in the first bar it will fit in, repeat until nothing is left. Longest first, because the big pieces are the hard ones to place, and leaving them until the end is what strands them in bars that are already half full. This is not the perfect answer, which is expensive to work out, but it gets close and it finishes while the cutter is standing there waiting.

Offcuts are then recorded with their lengths, so a leftover from one run is found and used by the next instead of going in the bin. The cutters run it themselves at the saw.

Production reports turned out to be .xls, not the CSV I had been testing against, so I added native .xls reading rather than ask the floor to change how they export.

Unproven. I have no measured waste reduction. The packing is demonstrably tighter and remnants that used to be scrapped now come back, but whether that shows up in monthly scrap tonnage has not been checked against a baseline. That is the number that matters and I have not earned it yet. The floor app's logic is covered by tests; its GUI has not been run at the saw.

Cycle count validation, rebuilt

The script reconciling daily counts had been trusted for a long time. It was wrong in four ways, all of them silent.

Office Scripts (TypeScript) · Excel · OneDrive

  • A dead fraction parser read "1/2" as 1, rounding every half count up with no warning.
  • Validation ran one direction only, so shrinkage and missed pulls never surfaced.
  • Batch IDs were recomputed per row, splitting one count session in two whenever it crossed a second boundary.
  • A crash window let a re-run duplicate rows already committed.

Rebuilt around an immutable key of ID plus batch time plus type. The old version keyed on row numbers, which drift as history grows, so a correction written today could land on a row counted last month. Then split into three scripts so the job could be handed to five people across purchasing, production control and receiving, and run for two weeks without me.

Unknown. How long those four bugs had been running, and how much count drift they left in the records. All four failed silently, which is why they lasted. I did not go back and quantify the damage.

CNC material logging

Material pulled at the CNC was written on paper and keyed in later, or not at all.

Standalone HTML · Android tablet at the machine

The first attempt was a Power Automate flow and it failed in practice. I replaced it with a single HTML file on a tablet mounted at the machine. The operator taps the part, it logs. No install, no licence, no server.

This is the only tool here that does not reconcile anything. The others reconstruct what happened from records written later; this one records the event at the machine, at the moment of the pull, so there is no gap to close afterwards. That is the more expensive thing to build and the direction I would take the rest of this work given the chance.

Measured by. It is still in use, which is the only measurement I have. For a floor tool that is not nothing, since anything the operators dislike gets quietly abandoned inside a week. Whether the logged data is accurate enough to reorder from is a separate question I have not answered.

Consumption forecasting tool

You give it a stock count sheet. It works out how fast each item is really being used, says what it expects next, and then checks how wrong it was last time.

TypeScript · JavaScript · runs in a browser, no server

Usage is worked out per working day, not per day on the calendar. If two counts are eleven days apart with a weekend and a holiday in between, only six of those were days anyone could have used anything. Divide by eleven and a fast moving item looks slow. The longer the gap, the more wrong it gets.

It also tells apart three things that look the same on a sheet and need different decisions: stock being used steadily, stock used now and then, and stock that has not moved at all. And when a count comes back higher than the one before it, that is a delivery arriving, not usage running backwards.

The part worth pointing at: after it makes a prediction, it goes back over the counts it already has and works out how far off that prediction would have been. So it tells you how much to trust it, instead of handing you one number with no way to judge it.

What was wrong with it. The sheet has one column for every day you counted. Some of those dates were written 12/15/2025 and some 12-12-2025, in the same row, because that is what the export does. The tool only recognised the ones with slashes and threw the rest away without saying so: 53 of the 128 counting days in one real file, 18 of 48 in another. It did not stop and it did not warn. It worked out usage from the six days in ten it still had, and reported the answer as confidently as if it had all of them. A tool that breaks is obvious. One that quietly uses half your history is not. It now reads every counting day, and I checked that on the sample file before saying so.

Two more things from the same read. A supplier's name was written into the version that actually runs, but not into the source file anyone would open to check for exactly that. And a rewrite I had assumed was the newer version turned out to be unfinished and unable to run at all. Both are written up in the repository rather than quietly tidied away.

Measuring things with a phone

Small instruments, built out of curiosity. No employer, no client, nothing to sell. They run in Scriptable on an iPhone. Most have been run once or not at all, because each needs a particular drive to happen, and I have said so under each one.

Deriving the radius of the Earth

A car odometer and two GPS readings are enough to measure the size of the planet. The arithmetic is the easy part. Not cheating is the hard part.

JavaScript · Scriptable on iOS

The radius falls out of one division: how far you drove, divided by the angle between your start and end points as seen from the centre of the Earth. The distance comes off the car's odometer. The angle comes from the two GPS positions by trigonometry alone.

The obvious convenience is to let the phone compute the distance between the two points instead of reading the odometer. I took that option out. The standard formula for it needs a value for the Earth's radius in order to return kilometres, so using it would quietly feed the answer back into the measurement. The odometer is the only distance source that does not already know how big the Earth is.

Every result carries an uncertainty, combining the odometer's resolution with GPS error, and results are averaged with more weight given to straighter drives, since a winding road adds distance without adding angle.

The part I would defend hardest. The accepted figure of 6371 km is not shown while you are collecting. It appears only in a separate review mode, and only after five runs. If you can see the right answer while measuring, you will stop when you land near it and keep going when you do not, without ever deciding to. Hiding it costs nothing and removes the temptation completely.

Run once. One measurement so far, on a drive from Montreal to Toronto, which is a long and mostly straight baseline and about the best conditions this method gets. Its own rule asks for five before it will show you the accepted value, so by its own standard there is no result yet, only a first reading. Getting the other four needs four more long drives, which is the honest reason it has not happened.

Height of a roadside pole, from a moving car

Two photographs, taken a hundred metres apart, are enough to measure something you never stop for.

JavaScript · Scriptable on iOS

Photograph the pole, let the car travel, photograph it again. The two GPS positions give the distance between the shots. Tapping the top of the pole in each photo gives two angles. Where the pole stands, and how tall it is, follow from those.

The useful part is that the height is worked out twice, once from each photograph, and the two answers are compared. If they disagree by much the result is marked poor and asks for a retake. It also checks whether your second tap sits where the first photo predicts the pole should appear, and warns you when it looks like you tapped a different pole.

Built, not yet proven. It works, and it has not been used in the field since it was finished. The camera's field of view and the phone's height above the road are fixed numbers for one phone in one seat, so it is calibrated for my car and nothing else. It has never been checked against a pole of known height, which is the step that would turn a plausible number into a verified one, and it is waiting on a drive where I can stop and measure one properly.

Payroll rate extractor

JavaScript · runs from a browser bookmark

My wife is an accountant. Her firm runs payroll for several care homes through a payroll website, and that website shows each employee's pay rate on screen but leaves it out of every file it will let you export. So every pay period somebody sat and re-typed numbers the system was already displaying. I wrote a small tool that reads those figures straight off the page as it is shown and saves them as a spreadsheet.

The awkward part was the paging. The list is split across many pages and the site reloads completely every time you move to the next one, which throws away anything a tool is holding on to. So it saves what it has found after each page, builds the spreadsheet only once at the end, and then wipes its own store, so one pay period cannot quietly leak into the next one.

Not adopted. She checked its output against the real figures and it was right, and the firm decided not to use it. I wrote it for free, because it was making her days longer, not as a job.

My Evil Diary

A horror fiction writing surface with no text box and no send button. The whole page is ruled parchment and you write onto it, so Apple Pencil works natively. The passphrase is hashed in the browser and only the hash reaches the server, so there is no identity check to talk past and no way to recover a forgotten passphrase. Those are the same property stated in both directions and the README says so, rather than promising a recovery it cannot deliver. Working. Private repository, so there is nothing to click.

fourier-trace

It has a test suite covering the CLI and configuration, and it has never been run end to end, because that needs a local machine with ffmpeg, cairo and pango. No output clip exists yet.

couple-space

Unfinished. The code is complete and it has never been deployed or run, because it needs a Firebase project and a Plaid account that only I can create.

RPS Arena

JavaScript · Cordova · GitHub Actions

Hundreds of rock, paper and scissors pieces are released into a physics arena and convert each other on contact until one type is left. The maths behind it, the Monte Carlo runs and the arena sizing, is written up separately. Unfinished. Packaged for Android and never submitted. The odds analysis ended up as development tooling rather than shipping, and the game itself scores flat.
